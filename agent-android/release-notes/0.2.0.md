This is the release where the Android agent stops being a thermometer and becomes something you
manage a phone with. 0.1.0 reported telemetry, enrolled, and answered exactly one command.
0.2.0 locates a device, inventories and blocks its apps, enforces blocked hours and screen-time
budgets, locks it, erases it, and updates itself.

**Update the hub first, to 1.108.0 or later.** Everything below is answered by the device but
asked by the hub, and two of the pieces do not exist on an older hub at all: the per-platform
release manifest this agent reads, and the hosted APK the provisioning code points at. A phone
on 0.2.0 talking to an older hub is not broken, it is simply never asked anything new.

**No device in the field will pick this up on its own.** Self-update arrives *in* this release,
so 0.1.0 has no updater to run. Every existing device takes 0.2.0 by hand, by MDM push, or by
being provisioned fresh. From 0.2.0 onwards the phone updates itself like the other two agents.

# What the device can now do

## Find it

A **Locate** button on the machine page asks the phone where it is and puts it on a map, on its
own page and on a fleet map.

**It is on-demand only.** Nothing is collected in the background, there is no tracking loop, and
the device holds no location history beyond what it was asked for. Every request is written to
the audit log with the operator's name, and **the phone shows a notification naming the person
who asked**. Somebody holding a company phone can always see they were located.

**Locating is its own permission.** It is not covered by the one that lets somebody reboot a PC.
That was a deliberate split: "may restart a machine" must not quietly mean "may find out where
an employee is". Grant it separately, to fewer people.

**A fix carries how good it is.** The map draws an accuracy circle and names the provider, so a
150-metre cell-tower fix never looks like a GPS one. Location switched off comes back as
*unavailable* with a readable reason rather than as a failure, because a failure is
indistinguishable from a phone that is simply off.

## See and block its apps

The machine page lists what is installed. From there you can suspend apps, fleet-wide or per
device.

**Suspending is not hiding.** The icon stays where it was, greyed out, and Android itself tells
the person *"paused by your organisation"*. An app that silently vanishes reads as a broken
phone and produces a helpdesk ticket and somebody attempting a factory reset.

**The enforcement outlives the agent.** Suspension lives in the platform, not in this app, so it
holds even if an aggressive OEM power manager kills the agent overnight.

**Two safety mechanisms you cannot switch off.** The agent refuses to suspend the launcher, the
dialer, settings, emergency dialling, or itself, and the hub cannot edit that list -- a policy
that suspends the launcher is a bricked device. And if a device has heard nothing from the hub
for a week, every restriction lifts. A phone that loses its hub must not stay locked down
forever.

**Packages it could not suspend come back named.** A policy that quietly failed on three apps
while reporting success is worse than no policy, so the console shows enforcement from the
device's own inventory rather than from what was sent to it.

**Deleting a policy releases the apps.** That is the release valve, and it is worth testing once
so you trust it.

## Blocked hours and screen-time budgets

Curfews and per-app daily limits, authored on the hub.

**The device enforces them, not the hub.** It holds the schedule, persists it, re-applies it
after a reboot, and evaluates it every minute against its own clock. A curfew at 22:00 holds on
a phone that has been off the network since lunchtime, which is the only version of this feature
worth having.

**A window that crosses midnight is one window.** 22:00 to 07:00 is a single night, and the
morning half belongs to the night that started the evening before.

**Screen-time accounting needs a permission no device owner can grant itself.** Usage access has
to be switched on by hand on each device, once. The agent detects whether it has it, tells the
hub, and the console says the device cannot report usage -- rather than showing zeroes, which
looks like a phone nobody touches.

**The clock is pinned to network time**, and budgets count elapsed time rather than wall-clock
time, so moving the date forward does not buy anybody an extra hour.

## Lock and erase

**Lock** locks the screen now. Low stakes, and the natural partner to locating a lost phone.

**Erase** factory-resets the device, and has no undo and no dry run. It is gated behind its own
permission, needs the machine's name typed out and checked on the server, is refused through the
generic command endpoint, cannot be saved as a favourite, and cannot be fired by a rule. All of
that friction is deliberate and all of it sits on the half that cannot be taken back.

You choose whether factory-reset protection survives the wipe. Cleared, the device can be set up
again by anyone. Kept, it demands the original Google account -- theft protection or a
self-inflicted brick, depending on whose account that was.

# What the console now gets right

**It stops offering a phone things a phone cannot do.** The device reports what it supports, and
the console hides the terminal, the file browser, the process list, firmware and backups, with
one sentence saying why. More importantly the **schedulers** check the same list, which they
never did before -- the pilot phone was queued a file backup within a minute of enrolling. Fixed
at the source rather than in the page.

**A machine that reports nothing is treated as unknown, never as incapable.** Every Windows agent
in the field reports nothing and keeps working exactly as it did.

# Provisioning

Fully managed devices are provisioned by QR code after a factory reset, and the hub now mints
that code itself.

**Upload the signed APK on the provisioning page and the hub does the rest.** It hosts the file,
derives the signing checksum by reading the APK, and puts both into the code. Previously that
meant hosting the file somewhere yourself, running a command-line signing tool, and typing a
43-character checksum by hand -- in front of the one operation in this product whose mistakes
are only discovered on a device that has already been wiped.

**The code refuses to be minted broken.** A partial payload still scans and still starts, then
fails minutes later on a phone with nothing on it.

**Treat a printed QR as a fleet credential.** It carries the enrollment secret. Re-uploading the
APK invalidates every code printed before it, which is the way to kill one that walked off.

# Self-update

The phone now takes its own releases, from its own manifest, signed with the same offline
release key as the other two agents.

**Each agent is offered only its own build.** Until now all three reported into one version
field, so the Linux box and the phone were pinned low purely to be ignored. The hub reads the
platform first, and the dashboard's outdated count is now counted per platform.

**The install is silent and the agent comes back on its own**, but only on a fully managed
device. A sideloaded phone that is not a device owner declines the update and says so, rather
than raising the system installer at somebody's lock screen.

**A build signed by the wrong key is refused twice.** Android refuses an APK that is not signed
by the same key as the installed app, and the agent independently refuses a manifest not signed
by the fleet's release key. Those are different checks: the platform's says *same author*, ours
says *the fleet approved this exact build*.

# The usual

**What you need to do:** update the hub, then install this on each device by hand or by MDM.
After that they keep themselves current.

**The version still reads 0.2.0, and that is not a beta marker.** The three agents are on three
separate version lines that are not comparable to each other. A phone at 0.2.0 is not behind a
Windows PC at 3.35.0.

**Two new kinds of personal data start existing here** -- where a device is, and which apps are
used and for how long. Both are listed in `SECURITY.MD` with what is kept, for how long, and who
can read it. Reading either needs the machine to be in that operator's scope, not merely the
capability. Usage has no fleet-wide view on purpose. FleetHub cannot give your workforce notice
that this is switched on; that part is yours.

**If something misbehaves**, the agent logs to logcat under the tag `FleetHubAgent`:

```bash
adb logcat -s FleetHubAgent:*
```

A device that reports fine but answers no commands is usually not a device owner -- the console
shows whether it is genuinely managed, and an unmanaged phone degrades to a clear "this device
is not fully managed" answer rather than an error. A short app list on an unmanaged device is
the expected answer and not a bug. A phone that stopped reporting overnight is more likely the
OEM power manager than the agent, and that is the first thing to check on a new device model.
